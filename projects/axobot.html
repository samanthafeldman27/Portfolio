Bayly Lab, Axobot

2023 Research Assistant

Cilia are tiny, hair-like structures that beat in a coordinated wave, but how their internal skeleton turns microscopic sliding into that wave is still an open question. In Dr. Philip Bayly's lab at WashU, I helped build Axobot: a mechanical model of a cilium scaled up to about 10,000 times life size, made from a chain of linked "bristlebot" segments. The goal was to test whether a proposed mechanical instability mechanism could plausibly reproduce that bending and beating motion at a scale you can actually watch.

I built on an earlier prototype from two labmates, improving the anchoring between segments, finishing the coupling, and getting the sliding surface to reset on its own instead of by hand, while tuning wiring, motor drive, and bristle stiffness. Our best configuration got one side of the model bending past 180 degrees, with a non-dimensional force in the same range as a real cilium, though getting it to oscillate back and forth the way the real thing does is still open work.
